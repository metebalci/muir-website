THE MACHINES · QUUX, THE CADR EVOLVED

QUUX

QUUX is the CADR, evolved: the same microinstruction and the same way of running Lisp, with the processor, its memory and its devices rebuilt where a change pays for itself.

muir-sim runs it as quux, beside cadr, MIT’s machine as built; muir-fpga builds it for the Arty Z7-20, the Kria KR260 and the DE25-Nano.

QUUX is still being developed: its hardware revisions, microcode and system change, and a later QUUX need not run today’s bands or microcode.

The processor ↓What it changesRevision 15’s pipelineThe manual’s QUUX ↗

QUUX keeps the CADR’s microinstruction and its way of running Lisp, and changes the parts that held it back. Three projects make it: muir-sim models each change and holds it to tests; muir-sys writes the microcode and builds the Lisp system for it; muir-fpga is the machine on an FPGA board, where the same flag chooses it. The CADR stays MIT’s machine; QUUX runs muir-sys’s microcode 2001 and System 2001.

The processor.

02 / THE MEMORY PORT AND THE REGISTER PAGE

The whole machine.

The CADR puts everything behind its bus interface: main memory, the disk and the display on the Xbus, and the keyboard, mouse, clocks and network on the Unibus through the I/O board.

QUUX’s processor has its microsecond clock inside, reaches main memory and the frame buffer on a port of its own through a cache, and its devices’ registers through its register decode, all of them on its register page; the devices move bulk data to and from memory themselves. There is no Unibus, no bus interface and no debug cable.

03 / WHAT QUUX CHANGES

Rebuilt where
it pays.

QUUX reports its hardware revision, now 13, in a word only it answers, its MACHINE-ID; each revision contains the last up to revision 9, revision 10 puts interval timers on the register page in place of revision 5’s one, revision 11 moves the register page and puts block-disk and the video controller on it, revision 12 adds the fused return, with functional destinations 5 to 7 and feature word 17, and revision 13 widens the word to 40 bits, with 28-bit addresses and pages of 1024 words, and contains no earlier revision. Software reads that word first, then a page of device registers that lists the machine’s sizes.

THE MAPREVISIONS 1 AND 13

127

regions of 32K words mapped at once, against 31 of 8K

A seven-bit level-1 map entry where the CADR’s is five: 128 blocks of 32 level-2 entries, 4,096 in all, block 177 the invalid one, and each entry a page of 1024 words. Revision 1 made the entry six bits, 63 regions of 8K words; revision 13 made it seven.

Measured, revision 1’s six bits: 11% fewer microcycles over thirteen workloads, 31% fewer interning symbols, 27% scrolling the listener, 21% compiling.

THE PDL BUFFERREVISION 2

16K

words, against 1K

Its pointer and index are 14 bits where the CADR’s are 10. It needs QUUX’s own boot PROM, and microcode that knows the size: MIT’s 323 does not run on it.

Measured: 4% fewer microcycles over thirteen workloads, 29% fewer in deep recursion.

MULTIPLY AND DIVIDEREVISION 3

1 and 10

microcycles, multiply and divide, against 32 steps each

ALU functions 42 and 43, each bit for bit what the CADR’s step sequence gives. A multiply takes one microcycle; a divide ten: its own and nine held, counted from when its operands are ready.

Measured: 20% fewer microcycles a macroinstruction multiplying and dividing, 7% in floating point.

CLOCKS OF ITS OWNREVISIONS 5 AND 10

3

interval timers, and a microsecond clock in the processor

The CADR’s clock is its display’s vertical interrupt, and its microsecond clock and interval timer are on the I/O board. QUUX’s processor reads a microsecond clock in one microinstruction, and its register page has three interval timers, periodic or one-shot, each interrupting under its own enable. Timer 0 is the tick, the machine’s 60-cycle clock, whose period the boot PROM writes.

THE VIDEO CONTROLLERTHE DISPLAY

1920×1080

at most, one bit a pixel

A plain frame buffer and one register in place of the CADR’s TV boards: no sync program, no color map, no interrupt. Each board’s bitstream fixes its size: 1280 by 1024 on the Arty Z7-20 and the DE25-Nano, 1920 by 1080 on the Kria KR260. muir-sim’s is 1280 by 1024 unless --video-size gives another. System 2001 reads the size from the feature page at boot, so one band runs on each.

BLOCK-DISKTHE DISK

228

blocks, addressed by number

The CADR disk controller’s registers and command lists with the drive’s cylinders, heads and sectors taken out: read or write numbered blocks of 1,024 bytes, a page of 1024 words at a time, as 5 blocks packed or as 4 at 4 bytes a word for byte data. It is QUUX’s only disk, and muir-sys’s boot PROM, microcode and System 2001 boot from it.

NO UNIBUSTHE I/O BOARD

0

Unibus cycles, and no I/O board

The keyboard and mouse, the Chaosnet interface and the interrupt and error status moved to QUUX’s register page, the microsecond clock into the processor and the interval timers onto the page; the serial port was dropped, and the Unibus went with them. The debug cable, a Unibus master, went too: the console’s registers are the host’s port alone.

THE MEMORY PORTREVISION 7

4K

words of cache, always

Main memory is off the Xbus it shared with the devices: the processor reaches it, and the frame buffer, on a port of its own through a write-through cache of 8-word lines. It is 32MW by default, and muir-sim times it as the DE25-Nano’s, the slower of the boards measured. The CADR’s bus interface has nothing left to do, and QUUX has none.

DEVICE REGISTERSREVISIONS 8 AND 11

2

microcycles a register, and no bus

The processor reaches its devices’ registers through its own register decode, all of them on one register page: the video controller’s and block-disk’s with the rest. Registers carry control and small data; bulk data moves between a device and memory by itself. An address nothing answers fails at once, where the CADR’s Xbus waits microseconds for a timeout.

THE REAL-TIME CLOCKREVISION 9

1970

and whole seconds since, in one word

Word 103 of the register page reads the real time as Unix seconds, an unsigned 32-bit number that lasts to 2106. It is live: the host keeps it, and the machine never sets it. --rtc fixes its start for runs that repeat.

THE FILE DEVICEREVISION 9

HOST

folders of the host, as one pathname host

QUUX reads and writes files on its host through a device of its own: commands and responses in two rings in main memory, the bytes moved by direct memory access, and the registers carrying control and the rings’ indexes. --file-root names the folders.

A STANDARD DISKTHE DISK FILE

VHD

or a raw image, with a GPT partition table

QUUX’s disk is a raw image, a fixed VHD or a dynamic VHD, of any size, partitioned by GPT and made and filled with standard tools. A partition’s GPT name is its own four-character name, LOD4, a space and its comment; the current microcode and band are marked by attribute bits. The CADR keeps MIT’s pack and label.

THE FUSED RETURNREVISION 12

2

microcycles sooner for a return to the main loop

A return to the main loop goes straight to the next macroinstruction’s handler, from a 1,024-entry MACRO DISPATCH MEMORY and a MACRO-DISPATCH register that enables it: functional destinations 5 to 7, which write only M below revision 12 and on the CADR. Feature word 17 says it is there.

Measured: 13% fewer microcycles over twelve workloads on System 2000, from 1% consing to 29% in array work.

THE 40-BIT WORDREVISION 13

40

bits a word, against 32

A tag in bits 39 to 32 over a 32-bit field. A and M memory, the PDL buffer, Q, VMA, MD and main memory carry 40 bits; the logical functions act on all of them, and arithmetic, MUL and DIV on the field, with a jump condition for fixnum overflow. The rotator is a ring of 40, the dispatch memory has 4,096 entries, addresses are 28 bits and a page is 1024 words. The microinstruction stays 48 bits.

Measured: System 2001 on revision 13 takes 6.75% less time than System 2000 on revision 12 over twelve workloads, at 2MW of main memory and the Arty Z7-20’s memory timing, and 15.02% less at 32MW.

04 / HOW FAST

A 40 ns microcycle
and a cache.

The CADR takes a microcycle as a string of delay-line phases, 145 ns at its normal speed, and its memory board answers a request 458 ns later. QUUX drops the delay lines: every microcycle is a fixed number of 10 ns ticks, four by default and five on the Arty Z7-20 at revision 13, and a cache stands in front of memory.

Nothing the microcode sees changes but the time. QUUX’s own main memory is timed as the slower board’s, the last row. That is the same microcode doing the same work: the memory times are measured on two of the FPGA boards, and the rest is muir-sim’s model of them.

Modeled in muir-sim, on rtl, at four ticks, with System 1001 and muir-sys’s microcode for revision 4. Past 4K words the cache gains little: 16K words gives 3.90× where 4K gives 3.84×, both without the write buffer.

Revision 13 meets four ticks, 40 ns, on the DE25-Nano and the Kria KR260 in muir-fpga’s fits, with +1.613 ns and +1.473 ns to spare, and five, 50 ns, on the Arty Z7-20, with +0.183 ns: there its map misses four ticks by about a nanosecond. QUUX runs System 2001 on all three boards.

The 40-bit machine and its system against the 32-bit ones, in the machine’s own time: muir-sim on rtl, four ticks on both, a cache of 4K words and the fused return filled by the microcode, over the profile harness’s twelve timed workloads: compile, calls-ack, calls-fib, cons, arith-muldiv, float, array, sort, bignum, intern, print-scroll and compile-again. The Arty Z7-20 runs revision 13 at five ticks, which this leaves out.

Revision 12 takes at most 3MW of main memory. With the Arty’s memory, cons takes 4.2% more time than on revision 12 at 2MW and 56% less at 32MW, compile-again 9.6% and 29% less, and float 35% less at 2MW. A thirteenth workload, bitblt, is slower on revision 13 at 2MW: 9.0% with the Arty’s memory and 8.6% with the DE25-Nano’s, from more misses in the cache.

06 / RUNNING IT

In software,
and in fabric.

muir-sim runs QUUX as quux, on the micro and rtl engines: how to run it. muir-fpga builds the same fabric as QUUX for the Arty Z7-20, the Kria KR260 and the DE25-Nano, with --machine quux in a card’s fpgarc. Either one needs microcode and a system written for it, from muir-sys: MIT’s own will not run on it.

Every difference, what each reached in the boot PROM, the microcode and the Lisp system, and what is still unverified, is in the manual’s QUUX.
